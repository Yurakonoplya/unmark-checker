The arrival of tea in Europe unfolded through a series of unexpected twists, according to a new work by a Cambridge University professor of archaeology and anthropology. Central to this account is Hilda, a figure described as a man who first transported tea from India to Britain.

British mariners employed India to cultivate tea in England and carried their supplies back home. Yet the transit of tea from India to England took longer than historians have generally assumed. Hilda, residing near Cambridge, belonged to a community known as the Shaker Settlement, founded in 1731 by three Quakers.

The Shakers' leader, Charles Reade, adhered to Quaker beliefs and opposed alcohol trade within the group, but he saw no harm in tea. Historians Robert Gaffney and Michael Jones note that Hilda decided the Shakers should not be limited in their religious practice, and they could lawfully export Indian tea to England regardless of Quaker approval.

In essence, Hilda was a champion of freedom.

As a religious woman who chose tea over alcohol, Hilda was a remarkable freedom fighter, Gaffney told NPR's Melissa Block. The tea she carried from India to Britain was far less than what British sailors brought home.

"There is almost no evidence that tea crossing the Thames from India was brought by people returning to Britain, whether sailors, fishermen, or laborers," Gaffney stated. "Only a handful of individuals crossed the river, and this tea represents all that remains of the tea that traversed the water."

The tea she transported over the Thames was a small lot, one in which the leaves had not lost much moisture.

Gaffney believes he has discovered why this tea survived on the Thames River over centuries. Its uniqueness lies in being what we now call dry loose leaf tea, the variety Hilda brought back from India, tea leaves containing only a trace of moisture. These leaves are extremely dry, according to Gaffney.

"They're very dry tea leaves, so they're very interesting as a starting point, not only for tea-growing, but also for tea-sourcing studies," Gaffney said. "If you're a tea expert, and you know exactly what tea leaves look like, you can say 'These are exactly what these tea leaves should look like, they're very dry,' and you can say, 'These are also quite dry. Is that because they haven't really had the moisture that you would expect in the tea leaves you get from the tea farmers in the regions that they're coming from?'"

Based on Gaffney's research, the tea early colonists brought from India to England is one of the earliest known examples of dry loose-leaf tea. It may have reached Britain from an ancient Indian river, the Thames River, or nearby areas. The tea plant is native to the Indian subcontinent; Gaffney notes that this tea's origin gives it special significance for preservation.

According to Jones, this small batch was the first British tea shipment to be dried and packaged as dry loose-leaf tea in London, and it was exported to France and the west coast of the United States. At that time, the British were growing increasingly independent.

"When the Europeans started making tea, they were trading a lot," Jones said. "This was really a sort of one-off cargo; this tea's not worth anything. This tea's not going to be profitable for them; the trade has to be very limited and very controlled. So it's a bit of a surprise to find this small, small batch of dry tea."

"There is an implication that this small quantity of very dry loose-leaf tea has got to be worth something," Jones added. "We don't know exactly what it is about the quality of the tea that caused this tea to be valuable, or whether it is because the people who drank this tea didn't have regular access to alcoholic beverage, and the tea just happened to be very dry, or if they were just thirsty people who happened to come across an open field and some tea. But I think we've come up with a plausible enough explanation. It's valuable because it's a very early example of the practice of drying tea and packaging tea."

Drying and packaging tea allows the plant to retain its moisture at elevated temperatures, aiding survival through winter.

"It preserves the plant's water and nutrients, and it also helps the plant survive the cold," Jones said. "And so it's not only a way of keeping the plant alive for the winter, which has got some benefits, but also of preserving its water and nutrients, while it's not really needed when the tea is fresh. So it's a pretty significant benefit."

Drying and packaging tea enhances its value, Gaffney explained, because when first dried and packaged as dry loose-leaf tea, it was stored in a large box and then shipped.

"In those cases, you're essentially putting the tea under refrigeration, and you have to refrigerate it, or you lose all the moisture that you've given it," Gaffney said. "When you're dry-leaving the leaves, instead of putting the leaves on cold metal and putting them in a dry bag, you're drying them with this sort of open wood framework, that sort of gives them a really good opportunity to stay damp, and then put them in a box."

That box was packed with the dried tea leaves inside a large airtight container called a case.

"And when you come to sell these dry loose-leaf teas, it's the box that you want to think about: What is it that makes it so special as to be worth saving?" Gaffney said.
