The story of how tea reached Europe involves a number of surprising events, including a man named Hilda who was the first one to take tea from India to the UK, according to a new book by a professor of archaeology and anthropology at the University of Cambridge.

British sailors used India to grow tea in England, and brought their tea home with them. But tea's journey from India to England took a little longer than what historians are accustomed to think. Hilda, who lived near Cambridge, was a woman who lived in a community called the Shaker Settlement, which was established in 1731 by three Quakers.

The Shaker community's leader, Charles Reade, was a follower of the Quaker religion, and was known to be against the trade of alcohol in their society, but Reade decided that tea wasn't a bad thing. According to historian Robert Gaffney and his colleague Michael Jones, Hilda decided that she didn't want the Shakers to be restricted in their practice of religious freedom, and that they could legally export Indian tea to England with or without the permission of the Quakers.

In other words, Hilda was a freedom fighter.

As a religious woman, who drank tea rather than alcohol, Hilda was a great freedom fighter, Gaffney told NPR's Melissa Block. And the tea she brought from India to Britain was much less than what the British sailors brought back.

"There is almost no evidence that tea coming across the Thames from India was brought in by people who came back to Britain, whether sailors or fishermen or labourers," Gaffney said. "There was just a handful of people who crossed the river, and this tea is all that remains of the tea that went across the water."

The tea she brought over the Thames was a small batch that was not in such a state that the tea leaves had lost much moisture at all.

Gaffney has found what he thinks is the key to tea's survival on the Thames River over the centuries. What makes this tea unique is that it is what we would call "dry loose leaf" tea, the kind that Hilda brought back to Britain from India — tea leaves with just a little moisture in it. These tea leaves are very dry, according to Gaffney.

"They're very dry tea leaves, so they're very interesting as a starting point, not only for tea-growing, but also for tea-sourcing studies," Gaffney said. "If you're a tea expert, and you know exactly what tea leaves look like, you can say 'These are exactly what these tea leaves should look like, they're very dry,' and you can say, 'These are also quite dry. Is that because they haven't really had the moisture that you would expect in the tea leaves you get from the tea farmers in the regions that they're coming from?'"

The tea that the early colonists brought from India to England, according to Gaffney's research, is one of the earliest known examples of Dry loose-leaf tea and could have come to Britain from an ancient Indian river, the Thames River, or from nearby locations. The tea plant is native to the Indian subcontinent; according to Gaffney, this tea's location gives it particular status as to why it is worth saving.

According to Jones, this small batch of tea was the first British tea shipment to be dried and packaged as dry loose-leaf tea in London, and it was exported to France and the west coast of the United States. At this point in their lives, the British were becoming more and more independent.

"When the Europeans started making tea, they were trading a lot," according to Jones. "This was really a sort of one-off cargo; this tea's not worth anything. This tea's not going to be profitable for them; the trade has to be very limited and very controlled. So it's a bit of a surprise to find this small, small batch of dry tea. "

"There is an implication that this small quantity of very dry loose-leaf tea has got to be worth something," Jones added. "We don't know exactly what it is about the quality of the tea that caused this tea to be valuable, or whether it is because the people who drank this tea didn't have regular access to alcoholic beverage, and the tea just happened to be very dry, or if they were just thirsty people who happened to come across an open field and some tea. But I think we've come up with a plausible enough explanation. It's valuable because it's a very early example of the practice of drying tea and packaging tea."

Drying tea and packaging it allows the tea plant to retain its moisture at elevated temperatures, which helps them survive during the winter.

"It preserves the plant's water and nutrients, and it also helps the plant survive the cold," Jones said. "And so it's not only a way of keeping the plant alive for the winter, which has got some benefits, but also of preserving its water and nutrients, while it's not really needed when the tea is fresh. So it's a pretty significant benefit."

Drying tea and packaging it makes the tea even more valuable, Gaffney explained, because when it was first dried and packaged as dry loose-leaf tea, it was stored in a large box and then transported by ship.

"In those cases, you're essentially putting the tea under refrigeration, and you have to refrigerate it, or you lose all the moisture that you've given it," Gaffney said. "When you're dry-leaving the leaves, instead of putting the leaves on cold metal and putting them in a dry bag, you're drying them with this sort of open wood framework — that sort of gives them a really good opportunity to stay damp, and then put them in a box."

That "box" was packed with the dried tea leaves inside a large airtight container called a case.

"And when you come to sell these dry loose-leaf teas, it's the box that you want to think about: What is it that makes it so special as to be worth saving?" Gaffney said.
