In the early days of railways, passengers were often provided with small, individual meals made from their own meal materials. These were generally made using a specially prepared, often time consuming and often messy dish. As this practice grew, there were several methods to prepare and serve these meals. While some of these methods made it possible for passengers to remain at the carriage throughout their journey, many of them were not especially convenient for the passengers, and they were less convenient for the railway officials as well.
These meal making systems were, therefore, replaced in the early 1900's by a more convenient system for serving meal material on a railcar. This was called a "barbeque style" service. The food was generally served in a rectangular tray on the outside of an open side of the car. Passengers in this system were then provided with enough room to sit or lie down and to cook their own meal, and they were served at least two meals during a journey. Generally, there was room to serve three meals if the food was being cooked on a wood burner in a grill.
Passengers in the modern style of railway service are fed by being moved from compartment to compartment by train conductors. The conductors do the cooking and serve the passengers in each of these carriages. This allows each compartment to be serviced every fifteen to twenty minutes, depending upon speed and train traffic, and, again depending upon the train's speed.
This way, each compartment is made much safer than it would be if there were no one sitting in it while the food was being served. There are also a number of advantages to the modern system. There are fewer train conductors, and there are fewer train officers to supervise the conductors. This allows the conductors much less work, and also allows them to concentrate much less time on the food.
In order to provide these compartment meals, a number of different types of cooking devices have been devised for use on railway carriages. These cooking devices range from the simpler individual microwave ovens in which food is placed on a wire rack inside the carriage, to very large, complicated and heavy ovens which incorporate a cooking deck and several ovens. In a typical railway carriage, it is common for the passenger to have as many as ten different types of cooking devices in his compartment at different times. The food is prepared, and served in the carriages, by a host of train conductors, and food preparation is handled by a number of train officers.
In some situations it is desirable to provide a food preparation area on a train car from which all of the food and cooking devices and personnel serving the food in the area can be conveniently and safely removed from the railcar and transported off the train. This can be necessary when the food must be served immediately after the train has passed through a long tunnel. However, in most of these situations, the necessary removal of the food from the kitchen area would be too time consuming and complicated for the conductors to do themselves, particularly if they were not carrying any of their personal cooking equipment.
There have been many variations and improvements upon the basic "barbeque style" system of railroad food preparation. Many of these solutions offer a convenient and safe food preparation area on the train car interior, or on the side or roof of the car. However, all of these previous solutions have required a considerable amount of space and require a significant amount of maintenance and supervision by the conductors to complete, especially when a large number of passengers are present on the car.
Thus, there is a need for a food preparation and storage area, and for a train arrangement capable of handling a large number of passengers in only a minimal amount of time. There is a further need for a food storage area that is not only conveniently located on the train but which is easily available and usable for the train.
The present invention meets these and other needs.
A problem which has developed which needs to be adequately solved is the problem of providing a relatively small amount of railcar storage while maintaining or improving the safety and convenience of the area by which food is prepared. The food storage area is an integral part of the train arrangement. This makes it necessary that a food facility be provided on all of the train cars. However, because there were so many different problems to be solved, any solution which would allow the storage area on one of the cars to be utilized for all of the other cars would have to be completely separate from the train arrangement.
Some types of railroad train arrangements do allow some food storage area to be used for other purposes while preserving or improving the safety and convenience of the food preparation area for the food service on all of the cars. In other instances, such as the system shown in FIG. 1 of the appended drawings, there are a number of food storage areas along the car that are separate from each other in a manner which improves the safety and convenience of the area by which food is produced. This is illustrated by the arrangement 1 in which the kitchen 3 and the storage compartments 9 and 17 are located along the interior track of the train car. The kitchen includes various cooking devices 3A that are used to produce food, while the storage areas 8 and 17 include food storage compartments 16 and 16A which allow various types of foodstuffs to be transported from compartment to compartment without requiring storage space in addition to the storage compartment on the outside of the car. This arrangement also allows some foodstuffs to be loaded into the food storage compartments 16 and 16A directly from the train car, and to be transported back into the compartment on another car without requiring further transport.
