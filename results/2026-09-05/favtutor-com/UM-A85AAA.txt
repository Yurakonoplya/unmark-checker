The narrative detailing tea's introduction to Europe is filled with unexpected occurrences, particularly involving a person named Hilda, who is noted in a recent publication by a University of Cambridge professor specializing in archaeology and anthropology as the first individual to transport tea from India to the UK.

British sailors cultivated tea in India, subsequently bringing their harvest back to England. However, the timeline for tea's transition from India to England was longer than what conventional historical accounts suggest. Hilda, residing in proximity to Cambridge, was part of a community known as the Shaker Settlement, founded in 1731 by three Quakers.

The head of the Shaker community, Charles Reade, adhered to Quaker principles and opposed alcohol trade within their society, yet he deemed tea as acceptable. Historian Robert Gaffney, along with his associate Michael Jones, reveals that Hilda opposed restrictions on the Shakers' religious freedoms, asserting that they could legally export Indian tea to England irrespective of Quaker authorization.

In essence, Hilda embodied the spirit of a freedom fighter.

As a devout individual who preferred tea over alcohol, Hilda was a significant advocate for freedom, Gaffney told NPR's Melissa Block. The amount of tea she transported from India to Britain was considerably less than what was typically brought back by British sailors.

Gaffney noted, "Almost no evidence exists indicating that tea shipped across the Thames from India was transported by those returning to Britain, whether they were sailors, fishermen, or laborers." He stated that only a few individuals crossed the river, and the tea they carried is the sole remnant of what traversed the water.

The tea she conveyed over the Thames was a limited quantity that had retained much of its moisture.

Gaffney believes he has identified the crucial element that allowed tea to endure on the Thames River throughout the years. This specific tea is classified as "dry loose leaf," which was the variety Hilda brought from India—tea leaves containing minimal moisture. According to Gaffney, these leaves are exceptionally dry.

"They're very dry tea leaves, making them intriguing not only for tea cultivation but also for tea sourcing studies," Gaffney remarked. "A tea specialist, familiar with the appearance of different tea leaves, can identify that these leaves are very dry and compare them to others, questioning whether the dryness results from insufficient moisture expected in tea leaves from the farming regions."

Research by Gaffney indicates that the tea transported by early colonists from India to England represents one of the earliest known instances of dry loose-leaf tea, potentially sourced from an ancient river in India, the Thames River, or surrounding areas. Gaffney emphasizes that the tea's origin, being native to the Indian subcontinent, contributes to its significance and the rationale for its preservation.

Jones asserts that this limited supply of tea was the inaugural British shipment dried and packaged as dry loose-leaf tea in London and was later exported to France and the west coast of the United States. During this period, the British were increasingly asserting their independence.

"When Europeans began producing tea, their trading activities were extensive," Jones explained. "This cargo was quite unique; it had little market value. It wasn't likely to be profitable for them; the trade was constrained and strictly regulated. Hence, discovering this minuscule batch of dry tea is somewhat surprising."

Jones added, "The notion arises that this small quantity of very dry loose-leaf tea must hold some worth." The specific quality that made this tea valuable remains uncertain, whether due to the consumers’ limited access to alcoholic beverages, the tea's exceptional dryness, or simply the happenstance of thirsty individuals discovering an open field with tea. However, a plausible explanation is that its significance lies in being an early example of the methods of drying and packaging tea.

The technique of drying and packaging tea assists in retaining its moisture during elevated temperatures, supporting the plant's survival throughout winter.

"It preserves the plant's moisture and nutrients, aiding its resilience against cold," Jones said. "Thus, it's not only about sustaining the plant through winter, which has certain advantages, but also about conserving its water and nutrients, which are less essential when the tea is fresh. Therefore, it provides considerable benefit."

Gaffney elaborated that drying and packaging the tea enhances its value, as the initial process involved storing the tea in a large container before transport by ship.

"In such instances, you're effectively refrigerating the tea, as failing to do so would cause it to lose moisture," Gaffney noted. "When the leaves are dried, instead of placing them on cold metal in a dry bag, they are treated within an open wooden structure—this technique aids in keeping them adequately moist before being placed in a box."

This "box" contained the dried tea leaves inside a sizable airtight container known as a case.

"When you consider selling these dry loose-leaf teas, the focal point becomes the box itself: What attributes render it so valuable that it warrants preservation?" Gaffney stated.
