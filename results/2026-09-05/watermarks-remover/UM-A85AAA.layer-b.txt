According to a recent publication by a University of Cambridge archaeology and anthropology professor, the arrival of tea in Europe was marked by unexpected occurrences. Central to this narrative is Hilda, identified as the initial individual to transport tea from India to the United Kingdom. While British naval personnel cultivated and carried tea from India to England, historical accounts have underestimated the duration of this specific trans-Atlantic journey. Hilda resided in proximity to Cambridge within the Shaker Settlement, a community founded in 1731 by three Quakers.

Charles Reade, the head of the Shaker group, adhered to Quaker principles and opposed alcohol trade within their society; however, he did not view tea negatively. Historians Robert Gaffney and Michael Jones note that Hilda rejected restrictions on religious freedom, asserting the group’s legal right to export Indian tea to England regardless of Quaker consent. Consequently, Hilda is characterized as a freedom fighter. Gaffney told NPR’s Melissa Block that as a religious figure who preferred tea over alcohol, Hilda embodied this struggle. Notably, the volume of tea Hilda transported from India to Britain was significantly smaller than the quantities brought back by British sailors.

Gaffney asserts that there is minimal evidence suggesting tea crossing the Thames from India was carried by returning individuals such as sailors, fishermen, or laborers. "There was just a handful of people who crossed the river, and this tea is all that remains of the tea that went across the water," he stated. The specific batch Hilda carried over the Thames was small and retained significant moisture, meaning the leaves had not lost much water.

Gaffney believes he has identified the reason for this tea’s survival on the Thames over centuries. Its distinctiveness lies in its classification as "dry loose leaf" tea—the same variety Hilda brought from India—characterized by tea leaves containing minimal moisture. Gaffney describes these leaves as exceptionally dry. "They're very dry tea leaves, so they're very interesting as a starting point, not only for tea-growing, but also for tea-sourcing studies," he explained. He added that a tea expert recognizing the visual appearance of these leaves would note their dryness, potentially indicating they lacked the moisture typically expected in leaves from their region of origin.

Gaffney’s research indicates that the tea early colonists moved from India to England represents one of the earliest known instances of dry loose-leaf tea. It may have originated from the ancient Indian river, the Thames River, or nearby areas. Since the tea plant is native to the Indian subcontinent, Gaffney argues that this tea’s specific location grants it unique significance worthy of preservation.

Jones notes that this small batch was the first British tea shipment dried and packaged as dry loose-leaf tea in London, subsequently exported to France and the U.S. West Coast. This period coincided with increasing British independence. Jones observes, "When the Europeans started making tea, they were trading a lot." He describes the shipment as a unique, low-value cargo that would not have been profitable, necessitating strict limitations and control. "So it's a bit of a surprise to find this small, small batch of dry tea," Jones remarked.

Jones further implies that this tiny quantity of very dry loose-leaf tea must possess value, though the exact cause—whether inherent quality, lack of alternative beverages like alcohol for consumers, or mere chance discovery—remains unclear. "I think we've come up with a plausible enough explanation," he added. "It's valuable because it's a very early example of the practice of drying tea and packaging tea."

Packaging and drying allow the tea plant to maintain moisture at higher temperatures, aiding winter survival. "It preserves the plant's water and nutrients, and it also helps the plant survive the cold," Jones said. He emphasized that this method not only sustains the plant through winter but also conserves water and nutrients, offering a significant advantage over fresh tea.

Gaffney explained that this process enhances the tea's value, as initial drying and packaging involved storing the leaves in a large box before shipping. "In those cases, you're essentially putting the tea under refrigeration, and you have to refrigerate it, or you lose all the moisture that you've given it," he noted. He clarified that instead of placing leaves on cold metal or in dry bags, the process involved using an open wood framework to dry them, allowing them to remain damp before being boxed. This "box" contained the dried leaves within a large airtight container known as a case. Gaffney concluded by highlighting the importance of this container in the context of selling such teas: "And when you come to sell these dry loose-leaf teas, it's the box that you want to think about: What is it that makes it so special as to be worth saving?"
