A river begins long before it looks like a river. High on a slope, rain and
melting snow gather in the soil until the ground can hold no more, and the first
threads of water appear between stones. These threads join others, cut a shallow
groove, and follow the land downhill. Where the slope is steep the water moves
fast and carries sand and gravel with it; where the land flattens the current
slows and drops what it was carrying, building banks and bars of sediment. Over
many years this patient work carves valleys, rounds the edges of hills, and
moves whole mountains grain by grain toward the sea.

People have always settled near rivers, because a river offers three things at
once: drinking water, a road, and a source of power. Early towns grew where a
river could be crossed by a ford or a bridge, and markets appeared at those
crossings because travellers had to pause there anyway. Mills were built where
the current was strong enough to turn a wheel, and the wheel turned stones that
ground grain into flour or hammers that shaped iron. A single fall of water,
used well, could do the work of many hands.

Reading a river teaches patience. Its level rises after rain far upstream, so a
clear sky overhead is no promise of a calm crossing. The colour of the water
tells you what it carries: brown after a storm, green over deep still pools,
grey where it grinds rock into powder. Fishermen learn to read the surface for
the swirl that marks a sunken log, and boatmen learn that the deepest channel
often runs against the outer bank of a bend, where the current has dug hardest.

When a river reaches the sea it slows for the last time and lets go of the last
of its load. The sediment spreads into a fan of low islands and shifting
channels called a delta, some of the richest farmland on earth, precisely
because the river has spent centuries carrying good soil there from far inland.
So the mountain that wore away upstream becomes the field that feeds a city
downstream, and the same water that began as a thread between two stones ends as
a wide, quiet mouth opening into salt.

Understanding weather is a related habit of attention. Weather is what the sky
is doing today; climate is what it tends to do over decades. A cold morning
proves nothing about the century, just as a single warm week proves nothing to
the contrary. The two are measured on different clocks, and confusing them is one
of the oldest mistakes in plain talk about the sky. To follow either well, keep
a record, compare like with like, and be slow to draw a large conclusion from a
small window of days.
